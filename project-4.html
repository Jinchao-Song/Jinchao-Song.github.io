Global Building Material Stocks

Mapping the materials in the world’s buildings to understand urban inequality, material efficiency and future demand.

The question

What shapes the material footprint of cities?

Population and built-up area tell only part of the story of urbanization. We asked whether material stocks could be measured for individual buildings worldwide, and how wealth and urban form relate to the materials used to provide floor space.

From geospatial data to building stocks

We combined building footprints, heights, floor counts and functions from multiple datasets, then applied material-intensity coefficients for nine construction materials. Machine-learning models helped examine associations with GDP, population density, roads and urban form.

The findings

An unequal material landscape, with different paths ahead

Uneven distribution of material stocks

High-income countries contain 46% of building materials while accounting for 15.7% of the global population. Per capita stocks in low- and middle-income countries are only 14–33% of those in high-income countries.

Material efficiency varies with urban form

Of the eight forms compared, dense development with uniformly low-rise buildings provides floor space with the lowest material weight per unit of floor area.

Future demand depends on development choices

In our scenarios, bringing national floor area per person to today’s global average requires 419 Gt more building materials by 2050 than maintaining current national levels. The most material-efficient urban form could use about 30% less material than the least efficient form.

Behind the paper

Making a global assessment possible

When we began the project in 2021, assembling building footprints, heights and functions at a global scale placed heavy demands on memory, storage, CPUs and GPUs. I tested different approaches before adopting a grid-based workflow that processed the data piece by piece.

We then linked the building database with GDP, population, urban form and block-level landscape patterns. This turned a global inventory into a way to investigate material-efficient urban development and future demand.

The implications

Connecting inventories with urban decisions

Building-level material accounts can inform material passports, construction-waste planning, retrofits and circular construction. In rapidly growing cities, decisions about urban form can shape material demand for decades.

Local conditions still matter

The results do not define a universal ideal city. Land scarcity may call for taller buildings, while low-rise expansion can add land and infrastructure demands. Some building attributes and material intensities are inferred; planning applications need to consider local land, climate and infrastructure constraints.
